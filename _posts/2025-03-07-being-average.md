"Average" is a myth we cling to, a comforting fiction that promises clarity in a chaotic world. It tells us there's a typical path, a median wage, a predictable outcome. But this illusion flattens reality. Life is not built on averages—it thrives in extremes, in the messy, jagged edges of individuality.

Statistically, you don't live at the average. You're not the median. The odds you calculate for yourself—success, love, longevity—don't come from the middle, but from your own choices and circumstances. The "average" is a useful abstraction for societies, not individuals.

## The Exceptional, Not the Middle

Take history: there was no average Roman citizen. A senator lived a world apart from a gladiator or a farmer. Evolution, too, is not driven by average organisms but by the exceptional—those who adapt, innovate, or survive against all odds. The first bird didn't fly because it was average; it flew because it broke away from the norm.

And yet, modern life presents a second myth: that being a generalist—a so-called "average" person across many disciplines—is easy or unremarkable. In truth, the generalist is a rare phenomenon.

## The Polished Stone

A jack-of-all-trades is no average figure. They are the polished stone: smoothed by experience, versatile enough to roll through life's unpredictability, unburdened by the rigidity of specialization. In biology, the coyote thrives not because it is the best hunter or the fastest predator, but because it can live anywhere, eat anything, and adapt to every challenge. Renaissance thinkers like Leonardo da Vinci were not defined by one craft but by their ability to bridge the gaps between art, science, and innovation.

Here lies the beauty of the paradox: **to reject the myth of average is to embrace the generalist, the adaptable one—a rarity in itself.** The modern world doesn't reward those who cling to the center, nor those who specialize themselves into a corner. It rewards those who can stretch, adapt, and thrive in multiple dimensions. The generalist isn't mediocre; they are extraordinary because they remain in motion, able to shift and reinvent.

## Ancient Wisdom for Modern Adaptability

In philosophy, Stoicism teaches us to master ourselves in any situation. Taoism reminds us to flow, like water, bending without breaking. To thrive in today's world is to live by these principles—not to aim for the false safety of "average," but to become durable, fluid, and endlessly curious.

So, leave behind both myths: that "average" defines you, and that being a generalist is easy. Instead, strive to be the polished stone—smooth, resilient, and endlessly adaptable. In a world of jagged edges, the one who can roll will always go the farthest.
